Core AI concepts: Contrasting generative AI and traditional AI
What do we mean by AI? Artificial intelligence is an umbrella term. It covers a wide range of techniques designed to mimic aspects of human intelligence, such as recognizing patterns, making predictions, or optimizing decisions. For years, most business AI fell into what we now call traditional AI. AI is newer, and it behaves very differently. Traditional AI, prediction, and decision support. Traditional AI is largely about prediction. You give it historical data, define an outcome, and it learns patterns that helps it predict or clearly classify future inputs. The output is usually structured, a number, a category, or a yes-no decision. Let's look at machine learning as an example. This kind of AI has delivered value for years in areas like forecasting, risk scoring and optimization and it's still incredibly important today. Let's look at generative AI, which is around creating new content. The clue is in the name. Generative AI generates or creates something new. Now beyond AI being used to classify or predict, we can use it to create new content based on patterns it has learned, and context we provide. Generative AI can help you create content in various formats, for example text, images, code, or structured data, based on the natural language instructions you provide. Now people who know what they want to achieve can get to an outcome much faster through prompting. It's always worth remembering that AI is a thought partner, and you are the thought leader. Why this distinction matters to leaders. With the exam and for real-world leadership, the key is understanding how these approaches differ in capability and risk. Traditional AI is typically used to predict or classify with specific predictable goals and constraints. It may require deep technical knowledge to implement and can support custom solutions. Generative AI is far more flexible, but that flexibility introduces new considerations around accuracy, reliability, and governance, which we'll explore later in this course. Here's a practical business lens. Traditional AI usually improves existing processes faster, cheaper, more accurate. Generative AI can be used to augment and amplify the impact of your knowledge and experience you already have. It supports thinking, drafting, summarizing, and decision-making at scale. That's why this exam focuses on strongly on leadership, adoption, and responsible use, not coding. In the AB731 exam, you won't be tested on your ability to build solutions, but you'll be expected to recognize when generative AI is the right approach and when traditional AI is sufficient. Keep this distinction in mind as we move into the modules class drivers and governance in the next sub-lesson.

AI models: Pretrained vs. fine-tuned models and where each applies
Now, let's move from AI concepts to AI models. Now that we understand the difference between traditional AI and generative AI, let's go one level deeper to the models that power them. AI models power both traditional and generative AI. How those models are trained and whether they are adapted has a direct impact on business outcomes. In this sub-lesson, we'll look at the differences between pretrained and fine-tuned AI models and discuss how to evaluate which approach best supports the outcomes you want to achieve. If pretrained models is like buying clothes off the rack and wearing it as is, fine-tuning is the step where the same item is tailored to fit your specific needs and context. So what is a pretrained model? Pretrained models are general purpose models trained once at massive scale, and reused across many scenarios without custom-specific training. Pretrained models have general knowledge and can perform well across many different tasks without additional training. For example, pretrained large language models like OpenAI's ChatGPT 5.5 are what enables tools like Microsoft 365 Copilot to summarize documents, draft emails, or analyze content immediately. Another example of pretrained models is the Azure AI Document Intelligence pre-built models. The AI models are trained to recognize and extract information from invoices, receipts, IDs, and forms, usually immediately without custom-specific training. Another example of pretrained models is the Azure AI Document Intelligence pre-built models. The AI models are trained to recognize and extract information from invoices, receipts, IDs, and forms usable immediately without custom-specific tuning. Let's look at the strengths and trade-offs of pretrained models. For leaders, pretrained models are attractive because they're fast to deploy and don't require building or training from scratch. The trade-off is they don't automatically understand your organizations, your policies, or your specific domain. That's why techniques like prompting, grounding, and retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, becomes so important. Topics we'll cover later. So what is a fine-tuned model? Fine-tuned models are adapted versions of pretrained models trained further to behave better in a specific organizational or domain context. Fine-tuning takes pretrained models and adapts it further using additional targeted data. The goal is not general intelligence, it's better performance in a specific context. This might include industry language, internal terminology, or specific tasks. For example, with Microsoft 365 Copilot, tuning a pretrained model can be further adapted using an organization's own data. So Copilot responds in ways that better match internal language tone and business context. Another example is fine-tuning models in Azure OpenAI. Organizations may fine-tune a model to perform better in a specific domain. For example, customer support, compliance, language, or internal knowledge workflows. This may improve accuracy to reduce token usage. So let's look at the strengths and trade-offs of fine-tuned models. Fine-tuned models can deliver higher accuracy and consistency, but that comes with increased cost and complexity. They require data governance, monitoring, and often specialist skills to maintain. That is why not every use case needs fine tuning. So let's choose the right approach. From an AI transformation perspective, when it comes to choosing the approach that is right for your use case, the principle is simple. Start broad and only specialize when there's a clear business need. For many productivity and knowledge work scenarios, pretrained models combined with grounding are sufficient and lower risk. Fine-tuning becomes valuable when accuracy, consistency, or domain specificity is critical. For the AB-731 exam, focus on decision-making, not implementation. You should be able to explain why a leader might choose a pretrained model versus a fine-tuned one, and what trade-offs that decision introduces. Cost drivers and ROI considerations
So why does cost and return on investment matter when it comes to AI decisions? As an AI transformation leader, your role isn't just to enable AI, it is to make responsible investment decisions. Unlike traditional software, many AI services are priced based on usage. That means costs can scale quickly if you don't understand what's driving them. At the same time, return on investment isn't about using the most advanced model, it's about applying AI where it creates meaningful business impact. So what drives costs in generative AI? There are a few consistent drivers behind generative AI costs. More capable models generally cost more to run. Higher usage means higher cost and more complex tasks, longer prompts, richer output, also increases consumption. On top of that, integration with business systems, data sources, or workflows can introduce additional costs and governance considerations. Let's look at understanding token-based consumption. Most generative AI services charge based on how much information is processed, both when you send it, and when you get it back. You don't need to calculate tokens for the exam, but you do need to understand the principle. More input, more output, more cost. This is why good design, clear prompts, appropriate model selection, and reuse matters from a cost perspective. But cost is only half the equation. alone doesn't tell the full story. So often when AI is discussed, leaders are talking about productivity, saving time or resources. It's natural to start with productivity but it's important to go well beyond it as well. Return on investment comes from what AI enables. Yes, saving time but also improving quality and allowing people to operate at greater scale. In many cases the biggest value isn't cost reduction, but capacity creation, freeing people to focus on high-value work that better serves the purpose of the organization. Responsible ROI balances cost, impact, trust, and long-term sustainability, not just short-term efficiency. Let's take an AI leadership lens on return on investment. From a leadership perspective, the best place to start is often high-frequency, low-risk work, things people do every day. That's where small time savings compound quickly. AI use will have the most impact when it's aligned with business goals, understanding the starting point and measure what changes. If a legal document usually takes three hours to produce and now it can be done in 30 minutes, what does that mean for the capacity of your team? Are they able to work on more customer work, increase billing for the firm? One firm took the opposite approach and passed the cost savings to their clients. Immediately, they became the most sought-after for that particular service. Commit to practice of continuous learning and improvement. For the AB-731 exam, remember that return on investment is framed in terms of business outcomes and adoption, not technical efficiency. Leaders should pay attention not only to metrics but to signals like confidence, adoption scores, and resistance, because these predict long-term value. For the exam you won't be asked to calculate costs, you'll be asked to recognize which factors increase costs and where AI is most likely to deliver value. Always answer from the perspective of a leader making a responsible, value-driven decision.

When AI adds value: Scalability, automation benefits, and recognizing appropriate use cases
So let's look quickly at why AI does not add value everywhere. A common misconception is that AI automatically adds value wherever it's used. In reality, AI can increase cost, complexity, or risk if it's applied to the wrong problems. AI adds value when it supports what the organization is trying to become, not just what it's trying to speed up. As an AI transformation leader, your role is to recognize where AI meaningfully improves outcomes and where it doesn't. Scalability. Where AI really excels. One of the clearest signals that AI will add value is scalability. When a task is repeated frequently across teams, departments, or the whole organization, small efficiency gains compound quickly. Or in some cases, like radiography, AI can be used to help bridge the gap when the demand for qualified experts far outstrips the availability in certain locations. AI allows leaders to scale capability and amplify the impact of their teams. Then there's automation benefits beyond cost reduction. Automation is often framed purely as a cost-cutting measure, but one of the most valuable contributions from AI is reducing the invisible mental load that drains energy and decision quality. In practice, freeing people from repetitive work so they can focus on judgment, creativity and decision-making is a hugely valuable benefit that may be difficult to quantify. As a leader, it's important to be able to identify and articulate this benefit and to protect it. It's all too tempting to keep loading staff up with more tasks, but that risks burnout and misses the opportunity AI-driven productivity creates to be more innovative with how people use their time at work. Your job is to recognize strong AI use cases. Strong AI use cases share a few common characteristics. First they tend to be knowledge intensive. a task where people spend significant time reading, synthesizing, or drafting content. Generative AI is particularly effective when it can help people work with large volumes of information, for example, summarizing documents or identifying patterns across data sets. Another signal of strong use case is decision support rather than decision replacement. AI works best when it helps people prepare, analyze, or think, but humans remain accountable for judgment and outcomes. From a leadership perspective, these use cases usually improve speed and quality, and at the same time, without removing human oversight. That combination, high information load, repeatable patterns, and supported decision making is a strong indicator that AI is likely to add value. Weak or risky AI use cases. Not every task is a good candidate for AI. Weak or risky use cases often share the opposite characteristics of strong ones. For example, one-off or low-frequency tasks rarely justify the cost, effort, or risk of applying AI. Another red flag is high-risk decisions without human oversight. If the consequences of getting something wrong are significant, legal, financial, or safety-related, AI should not be acting autonomously. Poor quality or unreliable data also increases risk. AI systems amplify what they're given. If the underlying data is incomplete, outdated, or inconsistent, their outputs will reflect that. Finally, scenarios that require absolute accuracy, where even small errors are unacceptable, demand particular caution. For the exam, remember that these are situations where leaders should slow down, apply governance, or decide where AI is not appropriate for them. So evaluating your AI opportunities. These questions can help leaders evaluate AI opportunities. Start by asking whether the task is frequent. Repetition matters because small efficiency gains compound over time. Next, consider whether the task benefits from speed or scale. AI is most valuable when it helps people work faster or operate at scale without adding proportional effort. Then ask what human oversight is needed. Strong AI use cases preserve human judgment and accountability rather than remove it. Consider risk and mitigation. What could go wrong and how could those risks be managed through oversight, governance, or design. Finally, think about the impact of the solution and how the saved time or created capacity can be best used. For AB-731, leaders need to be able to identify the most appropriate use of AI, balance value, scale, and risk from a leadership perspective.
