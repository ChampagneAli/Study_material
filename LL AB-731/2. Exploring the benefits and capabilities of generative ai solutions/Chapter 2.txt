As a leader, when you evaluate generative AI, the opportunities generally fall into two categories, efficiency and innovation. is about doing today's work better, faster, or at a lower cost. Generative AI excels at reducing cognitive overhead. This includes tasks like drafting, summarizing, analyzing, and synthesizing information. When deployed at scale, Generative AI helps organizations reduce digital debt, the accumulation of content, data, and processes that slow work down over time. Now let's look at efficiency, scalability, and automation. Generative AI solutions can support many users at once, delivering consistent assistance without the constraints of human availability. This makes them particularly valuable for high-volume, repeatable knowledge tasks. Standardized communications and reporting. First draft outputs that humans then refine. Think of generative AI as an augmentation layer that can offer a way to scale human capability without scaling head count in the same way. Let's look at innovation, expanding what teams can do. Efficiency is often the entry point, but innovation is where long-term transformation occurs. Generative AI enables people to explore ideas, test scenarios, and create artifacts without needing deep technical skills. This lowers the cost and risk of experimentation. Now, let's look at it from a decision-making and insight point of view. Another innovation opportunity lies in decision support. While it doesn't replace judgment, it can improve decision velocity, the speed at which the organization moves from information to action. Limitations and risks: “Hallucinations,” reliability, and bias
So, why do limitations matter for leaders? So far, we've focused on the benefits of generative AI. To lead responsibly, we also need to understand the limitations. Generative AI systems are probabilistic, not deterministic. That means they generate responses based on likelihood, not certainty. This creates risks that business leaders must recognize, even if you're not responsible for configuring or deploying the technology. For AB-731 exam, you're expected to understand these risks at a decision-making level, not a deep technical level. In this sub-lesson, we'll look at hallucination, reliability, and bias. Let's start with hallucinations, when AI sounds so confident, but is wrong. A hallucination occurs when generative AI produces information that sounds credible but is factually incorrect or entirely fabricated. This is not the same as repeating an error that already exists in a document. If incorrect information is present in your data, that is a data quality issue. A hallucination is when the AI invents details that are not present anywhere. occurs because generative AI is non-deterministic. It is designed to generate plausible responses based on the pattern and language, not to verify truth. When prompts lack clarity, or when the AI is not grounded in reliable data, the model fills gaps by generating what appears most likely. For business leaders, the risk is not that hallucinations exist, but that they go unnoticed. This is why human review, clear prompting, and grounding AI in trusted data is essential. These controls reduce risk and allow organizations to benefit from AI without over-reliance. Now let's look at reliability, consistency, and predictability. Reliability refers to how consistent and predictable AI outputs are when used repeatedly for similar tasks. With Generative AI, reliability is not guaranteed in the same way it is with traditional software systems. Generative AI systems are non-deterministic. This means that even when the same question is asked multiple times, the system may produce slightly different responses. Changes in wording, context, or available information can all influence the output. From a business perspective, this matters because generative AI is best suited to supporting work, not replacing controlled or repeatable processes. Tasks that require absolute consistency, precision, or regulatory certainty should always include human oversight and validation. The next we look at is bias, which is reflecting patterns already in the data. Bias in generative AI refers to systematic patterns in outputs that unfairly favor or disadvantage certain viewpoints, groups, or assumptions. AI systems do not develop bias intentionally. They learn from data created by humans, which means historical, cultural, and organizational bias can be reflected in the results. Bias can surface in subtle ways such as the tone of a recommendation, which options are prioritized, or which perspectives are omitted. These effects may not be obvious in a single response, but can become significant when AI is used repeatedly or at scale. For business leaders, the risk is not only reputational. Bias can affect fairness, trust, and decision quality, particularly in people-related or customer-facing scenarios. This is why bias mitigation relies on governance, diverse data, human oversight, and responsible AI practices. Leaders need to make sure that teams are aware of bias in the data and in themselves as they view outputs. Risk is contextual, not universal. It's important to note that these risks are context-dependent. Using generative AI to brainstorm ideas or summarize internal notes carries a very different risk profile than using it to provide regulatory advice or make people impacting decisions. A key leadership skill is the ability to assess where generative AI is appropriate and where additional controls, review, or alternative approaches are required. One of the most important principles to remember is that humans remain accountable. Generative AI does not remove the need for professional judgment, ethical considerations, and review. For business leaders, this reinforces the need to set clear expectations, guidance, and governance around AI use.

Prompting: How crafting inputs influences AI output quality
So what is prompting? Prompting is how we give instructions to Copilot or other generative AI tools. Every interaction with AI starts with a prompt. When people say AI gave me a bad answer, what they usually mean is that the instruction wasn't clear enough. Copilot doesn't understand intent the way humans do. It responds to what is stated and what is implied. If we are vague, Copilot has to make assumptions. If we are clear, it performs far more reliably. For example, asking summarize this document produces a very different result to summarize this document for an executive audience and highlight risks. Same tool, different outcome. Effective prompting relies on your communication skills to treat it like a conversation with a friend. Let's look at the components of an effective prompt. Microsoft's prompt guidance is very consistent. Effective prompts include a small number of core ingredients. You don't always need all of them, but the more you include, the better the output. At a minimum, Copilot needs to know what you want to do. From there, context helps. It understands why the task matters. Sources tell it where to look. And expectations shape how the response is delivered. A simple example would be asking Copilot to draft an e-mail. A stronger prompt would be draft a short e-mail to a customer, explaining the project delay using a professional and reassuring tone, based on the attached status update. That structure dramatically improves the result. Setting a goal and context. The goal and context are about setting direction before developing lots of detail. The goal tells a copilot what tasks you want done. The context explains why you need it and who is it for. Without context, Copilot defaults to generic responses. With context, it becomes far more useful. This is especially important in business scenarios where tone, audience, and purpose matter. For example, asking create a summary of the meeting is vague. Adding context like create a summary for a senior leadership team who are not present immediately changes what Copilot prioritizes. You get fewer details, clearer themes, and a more executive appropriate output. Now let's look at source and expectations. Source and expectations are how we reduce guesswork. Sources tell Copilot where to look. Expectations tell it how to respond. When Copilot is not grounded in specific documents, emails, or meetings, it has to rely more heavily on general patterns. That increases the risk of hallucination or irrelevant details. For example, instead of asking what are the key risks in this project, you might say Using the last two project status reports and recent team messages, list the top three delivery risks in bullet points. You're telling Copilot exactly where to look and how to present the answers in the most useful way for you. Iteration improves results. Prompting works best as a conversation, not a single request. The first response is rarely the final one and what's expected. So when you sit down to write a prompt, decide to give it the time to go back and forth a few times. Microsoft explicitly encourages follow-up prompting. You review the output, then refine it. You might ask Copilot to simplify language, change tone, add examples, or focus on a different audience. For example, after receiving a draft, you might say, make this more concise, or rewrite this for someone who has no context to the project. This iterative approach improves quality and reduces risk far more than trying to write a perfect prompt up front. Prompting is risk mitigation. Prompting is not just about productivity. It is one of the most practical risk controls available to organizations using AI. Clear prompts reduce hallucination by limiting ambiguity. Grounding Copilot in trusted sources improves reliability. expectations help reduce bias by narrowing interpretation. What prompting does not remove is the need for human judgment. Review and accountability still sits with people. Prompting simply improves the quality of what humans are reviewing. Strong prompting is a foundational capability for responsible AI use.

Grounding and data: Importance of relevant data and RAG
So, what is grounding? Grounding is the process of anchoring AI responses in real, verifiable data rather than letting the model generate answers purely from its training. Without grounding, AI is essentially guessing based on patterns it learns during training, which is where hallucinations come from. When Copilot is grounded, it retrieves information from sources you already trust, such as documents, emails, and meetings you have access to. The response is then generated using that retrieved content. A simple way to think about this is that Copilot retrieves your organization's information and uses it to answer questions. Grounding is what makes enterprise AI usable and trustworthy. So how does Copilot ground responses? In Microsoft 365, grounding is largely automatic. Copilot uses Microsoft Graph to understand context and retrieve relevant information from emails, documents, meetings, and chats. Importantly, Copilot only retrieves content the user already has permission to access. It does not bypass security or expose new data. Existing compliance sensitivity labels and access controls still apply. For example, when you ask Copilot to summarize a proposal, it doesn't guess what the proposal contains. It retrieves the actual document along with related conversations, if relevant, and then generates a summary based on that material. You may have come across this term of RAG, or retrieval-augmented generation. Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, is the formal name for this approach. It combines two steps. First, retrieving relevant information, and then generating a response using that information. This is important because large language models do not have access to up-to-date or your organization specific knowledge on their own. RAG fills that gap by supplying the model with relevant content at the time of request. In practice, this means Copilot is far less likely to invent answers when it can retrieve real data. It doesn't eliminate risk entirely, but it significantly improves accuracy and reliability. RAG and Microsoft Copilot Studio. In Copilot Studio, RAG is used to ground custom agents in enterprise knowledge sources such as SharePoint, OneDrive, Dataverse, or connected systems. The agent retrieves relevant information, then generates a response based on that context. It does not simply paste text back to the user. Moderation and governance layers are applied before the response is returned. From a design perspective, this allows organizations to control which sources are used, reduce risk, and ensure AI responses align with approved content and policies. Let's talk about grounding as a risk control. Grounding and RAG are core risk controls for enterprise AI. By grounding responses in trusted data, organizations can reduce hallucinations, Retrieving current and relevant context improves reliability by scoping sources. It's possible to limit unintended bias or drift. Grounding does not remove the need for human judgment. AI output should still be reviewed, especially in high-risk or high-impact scenarios. Grounding improves the starting point, not the final accountability.
